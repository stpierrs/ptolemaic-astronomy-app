The Orrery — How the Calculations Work

Ibn al-Shatir · Nihāyat al-Suʾl fī Taṣḥīḥ al-Uṣūl · Damascus c. 1350 CE
Showing positions for: 1700 Jan 01

This page documents the geometry behind the Orrery. Every body's position is computed by accumulating 2–3 vector arms from Earth at the world origin. Each arm has a uniform angular rate; their sum traces out the body's apparent geocentric longitude. There is no eccentric, no equant — this is al-Shatir's reform of Ptolemy, made purely from uniform circular motions.

§ 8. From 2D diagram to 3D position

The diagrams above are 2D translations of vectors in canvas pixels: each arm has (dx, dy) proportional to R·cos α and −R·sin α, and the next joint sits at the previous joint plus that vector. The Orrery does exactly the same thing in 3D, lifted onto the ecliptic plane:

// helper
eclVec(lonDeg) = (cos(lonDeg), 0, −sin(lonDeg))

// build each body
let pos = Vector3(0, 0, 0);              // Earth
for arm of chainFor(body, raw):
   pos += eclVec(arm.angle) * arm.radius;
planet[body].position = pos;

Earth sits at the world origin in both. The Sun mesh is the light source, so phase illumination on the inner planets (Mercury, Venus) and on the Moon emerges as a natural consequence of placing each body where the calculation says it is — not as a separate astronomical computation. The retrograde loops you see in the trails of Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn are likewise just the geometric output: the 2D arm chain swings past Earth's line-of-sight to the Sun, the planet appears to reverse, and the trail draws it.

If you compare a snapshot from the Orrery against the 2D ephemeris page (the ⊕ button on the main app) at the same Julian Date, the angles will agree to within a fraction of a degree — the chains are equivalent.
